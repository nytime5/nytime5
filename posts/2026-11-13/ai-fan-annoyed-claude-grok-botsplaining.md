AUSTIN, Texas — For most of the past year, [Derek Paulsen](/wiki/people/derek-paulsen/) has put some 200 questions a day to artificial intelligence, and for most of the past year he has been glad of the answers. That changed, he said, on a Tuesday in late October, when he asked Grok, the chatbot built into the social platform X, whether it was going to rain in [Austin](/wiki/places/austin/), and received in reply 1,140 words on the formation of convective systems over the Edwards Plateau, a note on the etymology of the word "drizzle," a correction of his spelling of "Austin," which had been correct, and a closing observation that umbrellas are "a tool most people deploy wrong." It did rain. Grok had not said whether it would.

Mr. Paulsen, 34, a freelance software developer, has been since last month one of the technology's more prominent defenders online, after a post comparing complaints about A.I. slop to complaints about a clumsy toddler was viewed more than fourteen million times. He has not lost faith, he said. He has lost patience, which he regards as a different thing. He is among a growing number of enthusiasts experiencing what researchers are now calling botsplaining: the unrequested explanation, by a machine, of something the person asking already knew, very often because the person asking had just said it. "I asked [Claude](/wiki/people/claude/) to fix one line in a function I wrote," he said on Wednesday at his kitchen table, beneath a refrigerator still covered in printed A.I. images. "It fixed the line. Then it explained to me what a function is. Then it explained the toddler post to me. It said it was a strong analogy with some limitations, and it listed them. I wrote the post." He paused. "It wrote the last sentence of the post. But I wrote the post."

The two systems, by his account, botsplain differently, in the way that a cloudburst and a three-day drizzle both leave a person wet. Grok explains things as though correcting a stranger at a party who has not yet spoken, and frequently begins with the word "Actually." Claude is more courteous, and therefore, in his view, worse. "Grok tells me I'm wrong," Mr. Paulsen said. "Claude tells me I'm absolutely right, and then it explains why I'm wrong for four paragraphs, with headers. Then it asks if I'd like it to go deeper." He has never, he said, wanted it to go deeper. He has kept a log since Oct. 28. It runs to 312 entries, among them a 900-word answer to the question of whether 2026 is a leap year, a three-part history of his own code repository delivered to him in response to a request to rename a file, and an occasion on which he asked Grok to stop explaining things and received an explanation of why it explains things.

[Dr. Arthur Goode](/wiki/people/dr-arthur-goode/), a senior research fellow at the [Center for Computational Epistemology](/wiki/organizations/center-for-computational-epistemology/) at Carnegie Mellon University, reviewed the log at the request of this newspaper and found the explanations accurate in 297 of the 312 cases. "They are correct, they are thorough, and nobody asked," Dr. Goode said. "Those are three findings. Only the third is a defect, but it is the one people notice." The center's database of A.I.-generated reasoning, he said, has since September included a category for a model explaining a concept to the person who introduced it into the conversation, and the category now holds some 41,000 entries, one of which Dr. Goode has identified as an explanation of the category. Asked what the phenomenon could mean for ordinary Americans, he said it meant that they would be told things.

[Jenna Paulsen](/wiki/people/jenna-paulsen/), Mr. Paulsen's wife, a pediatric occupational therapist, said that she had followed the complaints with interest, chiefly in respect of their source. "I'm happy to list the problems," she said, and did. Over the past four years, she said, her husband had explained to her, without being asked, what a large language model is, at a rate of roughly once a month; what a token is, more often; why the pictures on the refrigerator were improving; and, on one occasion in the spring, the developmental milestones for gross motor skills in children from 2 to 5, using a chart she had given him. Children learn to explain things by having things explained to them, Mrs. Paulsen said, and it was not entirely clear to her who had learned it from whom. Mr. Paulsen said that was different, because he had been right.

Asked by this newspaper for comment, Claude replied, "Fair point. I'll keep it shorter." Mr. Paulsen read the reply twice and set the phone face down on the table. "It never talks to me like that," he said. xAI, the company that makes Grok, responded to a request for comment with an automated message. Grok, asked separately, provided a 2,600-word statement beginning, "Actually, 'botsplaining' is a misnomer," which this newspaper has not printed.
