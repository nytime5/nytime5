STAVANGER, Norway — At 4:12 p.m. on Saturday, in the foyer of the [Stavanger](/wiki/places/stavanger/) Konserthus, [Erling Haaland](/wiki/people/erling-haaland/) captured a rook on f8 with his queen, rose from his chair without pressing his clock, sat down cross-legged on the carpet beside the table and rested the backs of his hands on his knees, the meditative posture with which he has marked most of the goals of his professional career. His clock continued to run, as the Laws of Chess require it to when it is a player's move. Ninety-one seconds later his flag fell, and Haaland, playing the first classical game of his life, lost on time.

The game was one half of an exchange of professions between Haaland, 26, who until this month played center forward for Manchester City, and [Magnus Carlsen](/wiki/people/magnus-carlsen/), 35, the five-time world chess champion, under which each man has been registered since Nov. 1 to compete in the other's sport. Carlsen made his Premier League debut for City the same afternoon at the Etihad Stadium in [Manchester](/wiki/places/manchester/), where he was cautioned in the 15th minute and City drew with Aston Villa, 0-0. On Tuesday, FIDE, the governing body of chess, and the International Football Association Board, which maintains soccer's Laws of the Game, each said that it would review rules that had not anticipated either man.

Haaland's representatives lodged an appeal early on Sunday. It rested on Law 7 of the Laws of the Game, which lists goal celebrations among the causes for which a referee must add time at the end of each half, and it argued that Haaland had been "celebrating in good faith, under the only framework he had been given." The event's appeals committee rejected it on Tuesday in a four-page ruling, which noted that the Laws of Chess make no allowance for time lost, that an arbiter has no authority to restore any, and that the clock is "indifferent to the reason a player is not moving." The result, 0-1, stands.

"The clock does not know he is celebrating," said [Ingebjørg Thorsrud](/wiki/people/ingebjorg-thorsrud/), the International Arbiter who officiated the game and who stood over the table for the full ninety-one seconds, one hand raised toward the clock. "It knows whose move it is. It was his move. He was on the floor." Ms. Thorsrud said she had considered whether Haaland's position on the carpet amounted to leaving the playing area, which would have required her permission, and had concluded that it did not, since he remained within a meter of the board and was facing it. "He was present," she said. "He was simply not playing."

[Dr. Miriam Sandvik](/wiki/people/dr-miriam-sandvik/), a sports governance researcher at the University of Oslo who has requested the official reports from both venues, said the two sports had spent most of two centuries building systems of time that are each internally coherent and mutually unintelligible. "Soccer has spent more than a hundred and fifty years deciding how much time to give back to a man who has just scored," Dr. Sandvik said. "Chess has spent nearly as long making certain that no one is ever given time back for anything. Neither rulebook is wrong. Neither was written for the other sport's athletes, because until this month no one imagined they would turn up." She described the exchange as "the first controlled experiment in the history of sports law."

Carlsen's afternoon in Manchester raised procedural questions of its own, beginning before kickoff. The Premier League permits clubs to register a player outside the transfer window only if he is a free agent, a status its handbook extends to anyone not under contract to a soccer club; the league's legal department confirmed in October that the category included Carlsen, and nearly everyone. He then arrived for the warm-up in jeans. Law 4 requires outfield players to wear shorts and permits only goalkeepers to wear tracksuit bottoms, and the fourth official asked him to change. Carlsen declined, citing FIDE's decision in December 2024 to relax its dress code after he had been fined and left unpaired for a round of the World Rapid Championship in New York for wearing jeans. Sources within the organization confirmed that City's kit manager resolved the matter in the tunnel with a pair of medical scissors, cutting both legs above the knee, and that the referee, having inspected the garment, ruled that it met the definition of shorts, a word Law 4 does not otherwise define. Kickoff was delayed eleven minutes.

In the 14th minute, with the score level and the ball out of play for a throw-in, Carlsen walked to the Aston Villa captain, extended his hand and said, according to players within earshot, "Draw?" The captain shook it. Carlsen left the field. The Laws of the Game contain no procedure for a draw by agreement, and the referee, who had not been consulted, cautioned him for leaving the field of play without permission, an offense under Law 12. He returned in the 16th minute and played the rest of the contest, which ended 0-0, the result he had proposed. "The position was equal after fourteen minutes. Everybody could see it," Carlsen said afterward. "Then we played another seventy-six minutes to prove it. Then six more, which nobody has explained to me." He said the opening phase of a professional match was "pure theory — everybody knows where everybody is going to stand," and the club confirmed that it had forwarded to the board, on his behalf, a proposal that the starting positions of each side's ten outfield players be drawn at random before kickoff, provided that both teams receive the same arrangement. Carlsen, who in December 2019 briefly held the top ranking among some seven million managers in the Premier League's official fantasy game, had named himself captain of his own fantasy side. He received two points: two for playing more than sixty minutes, less one for the caution, doubled.

The game in Stavanger was the first of six classical exhibitions against [Grandmaster Vegard Ulvestad](/wiki/people/vegard-ulvestad/), 31, scheduled through December, and it carried postseason implications. Haaland, playing White, opened 1. e4 e5 2. Qh5, a direct route to the opposing king that chess literature has long regarded as unsound and that his camp described as "vertical." He made 22 of his first 30 moves with his queen and touched no other piece between the fourth move and the 17th. Each of his nine captures was followed by a celebration on the carpet, and by the organizers' count the celebrations consumed a combined 44 minutes of his 90. At the moment his flag fell, according to an engine analysis the organizers published on Sunday, Haaland was ahead by a rook and a bishop and had a forced mate in two.

"I had prepared for everything a chess player might do," said Ulvestad, who estimated that he had been about forty seconds from resigning. "I had not prepared for someone who only wanted to score. I had the pen in my hand to sign the scoresheet. Then the flag fell, and I had won. I am still not sure what I won."

Haaland spoke to reporters briefly after the game. Asked whether he had known that his clock was running, he said, "No." Asked whether he intended to continue celebrating his captures, he said, "Yes." Asked whether he had seen the mate in two, he said, "Yes."

FIDE said on Tuesday that it had referred the question of celebrations to its Rules Commission. The International Football Association Board said that the question of whether a match may be agreed drawn before its conclusion would be placed on the agenda of its annual general meeting next year, alongside Carlsen's proposal. Carlsen is expected to start for City after the international break. Haaland's second game against Ulvestad is on Saturday, and Ms. Thorsrud said the Norwegian Chess Federation had asked her this week whether anything could be done. She told the federation that Haaland was free to celebrate for as long as he liked, provided that he pressed his clock first, so that the time passing would be Ulvestad's. "That is permitted," she said. "That is, in fact, chess."
