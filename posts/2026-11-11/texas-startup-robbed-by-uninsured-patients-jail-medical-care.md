TYLER, Texas — The cash register at the Remand Health storefront on South Broadway Avenue opens each morning with $812 in it, a figure the company arrived at after eighteen months of legal research. It is $62 above the threshold at which theft becomes a Class A misdemeanor under the Texas Penal Code, punishable by up to a year in county jail, and a year, the company's clinical staff have determined, is sufficient for most orthopedic procedures, rehabilitation included. By noon on Tuesday the register had been emptied twice. Both clients were in custody within six minutes. One of them, a 61-year-old fence contractor with a torn rotator cuff, was examined by a physician on Wednesday afternoon, the first time, he said, that a doctor had looked at his shoulder since 2021.

[Remand Health](/wiki/organizations/remand-health/), which is based in [Tyler](/wiki/places/tyler-texas/) and said on Monday that it had closed a $14 million Series A round led by [Fullspeed Capital](/wiki/organizations/fullspeed-capital/), is not in the retail business. Its storefront stocks a modest inventory of snacks, motor oil and cigarettes, but the merchandise is, in the language of the company's pitch deck, "a set of price points." The service is being stolen from. The customers are uninsured Texans who have concluded, on the evidence, that the most reliable way to see a doctor in the United States is to be arrested first.

The business rests on *Estelle v. Gamble*, the 1976 Supreme Court decision holding that deliberate indifference to the serious medical needs of prisoners violates the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. The ruling obliges every jail and prison in the country to provide medical care to the people it holds. No corresponding obligation extends to the people it does not hold, a category that includes the more than 26 million Americans without health insurance, roughly five million of whom live in Texas, which has the highest uninsured rate in the nation and has not expanded Medicaid. The case itself began in Texas, with an inmate injured by a falling bale of cotton on a prison work detail, a fact that [Wade Castleberry](/wiki/people/wade-castleberry/), Remand's founder and chief executive, cites as evidence that the company is "bringing the precedent back to its home market."

Mr. Castleberry, 44, spent fourteen years as a prior-authorization manager at a large health insurer, where he estimates that he denied or delayed some 40,000 requests for treatment. "Every one of those people was eligible for care somewhere," he said on Tuesday, seated on a stool behind the counter, out of the line of sight of the register. "There are about 1.9 million people in this country with a constitutional right to a doctor. I never put that in the denial letter, because it wasn't in the template."

Prospective clients enroll online and complete what the company calls a clinical-legal intake: a single appointment with a nurse practitioner and a criminal defense attorney, both on Remand's payroll, who together match the client's diagnosis to an offense whose expected period of confinement covers treatment and recovery. The company keeps the matches in a table it calls the formulary. A dental extraction is a Class B misdemeanor, achieved with a $140 carton of cigarettes and good for up to 180 days. A hernia repair or a rotator cuff is the register. The company uses the word "robbery" in its marketing; its attorneys do not. Robbery under Texas law requires force or the threat of force and carries a sentence of two to 20 years, a range Remand's clinical staff regard as overtreatment for anything short of an organ transplant, and it is not offered at the Broadway location. Weapons are prohibited by the terms of service. Clients plead guilty at arraignment and decline probation, which Mr. Castleberry described as "the leading cause of loss of coverage." Smith County prosecutors, who said they had not previously encountered defendants requesting the maximum sentence, are reviewing how to oppose it.

The model has one structural vulnerability, which Mr. Castleberry acknowledges. A theft to which the owner consents is not a theft. "The entire enterprise depends on its customers committing a real crime against a company that wants them to," said [Professor Sandra Kittredge](/wiki/people/sandra-kittredge/), who teaches criminal law and health law at the University of Texas School of Law. Remand's solution is that its clerks are not told which customers are enrolled, and its board has adopted a standing resolution opposing all thefts from its premises. "The company does not consent," Mr. Castleberry said. "It anticipates." Professor Kittredge said she had read the resolution and found it sound. "They haven't found a loophole," she said. "The Eighth Amendment is the only provision of the Constitution that guarantees an American a doctor, and it has an eligibility requirement. They've read the eligibility requirement."

Remand does not charge its clients. Its revenue comes from two regional hospital systems, which it declined to name, that pay the company $1,150 for each uninsured patient it moves out of their emergency departments and into county custody. The economics are not complicated. An uninsured patient is, to a hospital, uncompensated care. The same patient in custody is driven to the same hospital under guard, operated on by the same surgeon in the same operating room, and billed to Smith County, which pays. "Nothing about the medicine has changed," Mr. Castleberry said. "We changed the payer."

[Royce Pickens](/wiki/people/royce-pickens/), the fence contractor, was quoted $31,000 in 2023 for the repair of his shoulder by the hospital where he is now scheduled to receive it, on Dec. 2, at county expense. "Same doctor," he said in a telephone interview from the Smith County jail. "He remembered me. He said, 'You're the shoulder.' I said yes sir, I'm the shoulder." Mr. Pickens described the theft as well organized, though he noted that the clerk had thanked him. "I been putting this off five years," he said. "The free clinic had a list. The hospital gave me a bill and an ice pack. Remand had a list too, but it moved."

The cost has fallen to the county, an externality its commissioners were not consulted about. The Smith County jail's medical budget of $6.1 million for the fiscal year has been exceeded by $1.4 million since Remand opened in June, and the jail has added a second orthopedic clinic day. "We are constitutionally obligated to provide care to every person in our custody, and we do," said [Lt. Bonnie Trammell](/wiki/people/bonnie-trammell/), a spokeswoman for the Smith County Sheriff's Office. "We are not obligated to provide care to anyone outside our custody, and we don't. I'm told that's the problem. I'm told it's been the problem for a while." The Smith County Commissioners Court has scheduled a December hearing on a proposal to fund a public clinic for uninsured residents, which the county auditor estimates would cost less than this year's overage. The proposal has been opposed on the grounds that it would extend public health care to people who have not committed a crime, a precedent one commissioner described as "very hard to walk back."

[Brent Haskell](/wiki/people/brent-haskell/), the Fullspeed general partner who led the round, said the [Menlo Park](/wiki/places/menlo-park/) firm, which is known principally for its stakes in frontier artificial intelligence laboratories, had been drawn to Remand's matching engine, a model trained on the Texas Penal Code and eleven years of Smith County sentencing records, and to its payer. "Every health-care company spends its first five years finding someone to pay," Mr. Haskell said. "This one has a payer the Supreme Court found for it in 1976, and the payer isn't allowed to say no. It's in the Constitution. That's an A."

The waiting list to steal from the Broadway location stands at 2,300 people and eleven weeks, which Mr. Castleberry noted is shorter than the wait for a new-patient appointment at the county's free clinic. It is the clearest revealed preference in East Texas health care. The company plans to open four more locations in Texas and two in Florida in 2027, each in a county without a public hospital. "People say we're exploiting a gap in the system," Mr. Castleberry said. "There's no gap. The line is very clearly drawn. We help people across it."

At 3:40 p.m. on Tuesday, a man in a back brace came in, bought a bottle of water and a packet of ibuprofen, paid, and left. Mr. Castleberry watched him through the window as he walked slowly to his truck. "He's not enrolled," he said. "He's just a customer."
