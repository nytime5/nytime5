AUSTIN, Texas — The refrigerator in Derek Paulsen's kitchen is a white French-door model of the kind sold by the thousands across central Texas, and it is covered, very nearly to the hinges, in pictures. There is a horse with five legs, grazing contentedly. There is a Thanksgiving table at which every guest is the same smiling man. There is a birthday card for someone named "Jenan," a map of the United States showing fifty-three states, two of them Ohio, and a portrait of a golden retriever whose tongue continues past the bottom edge of the print and onto a second sheet, which has been taped beneath the first so that the tongue can finish. Each is held in place by a magnet. None was made by a child. The Paulsens do not have children. It is, by any measure, a proud refrigerator.

[Derek Paulsen](/wiki/people/derek-paulsen/), 34, a freelance software developer on the east side of [Austin](/wiki/places/austin/), printed every one of the pictures himself from images generated by artificial intelligence, and he regards the refrigerator as the plainest statement of a position that has made him, over the past three weeks, one of the more widely quoted defenders of the technology online. "Complaining about A.I. slop is like complaining that a toddler is clumsy," he wrote in a social media post on Oct. 16 that has since been viewed more than fourteen million times and was shared by, among others, the venture investor [Brent Haskell](/wiki/people/brent-haskell/), who added the word "This." "It's learning to walk. You don't yell at a toddler for falling down. You put the drawing on the fridge."

Mr. Paulsen was the subject of [an article in this newspaper in March](/2026-03-20/man-vibe-coding-session-hallucination-own-instructions/), after a fourteen-hour session of A.I.-assisted programming left him unable to say whether he had written the instructions he was following. He went back to writing code by hand afterward, a period that lasted, by his account, nine days. "I came back because it was better, and because it was getting better, and those are two different reasons," he said on Thursday at his kitchen table, in words very close to those. He dates the technology's birth to the public release of the first widely used chatbot, in November 2022, which makes it, he noted, 4 years old this month. "Think about what you could do at 4," he said. "Now think about what it can do at 4."

[Jenna Paulsen](/wiki/people/jenna-paulsen/), his wife, has been thinking professionally about what children can do at 4 for eleven years. A pediatric occupational therapist at a practice in South Austin, she has evaluated, by her estimate, some 1,400 children for delays in gross motor development, and she described her husband's comparison, without looking up from her coffee, as "inexact in several respects I'm happy to list." The first was age. "Most children have stopped falling down regularly by 4," she said. "If a 4-year-old came to me falling down as often as this does, I would not tell the parents to put the drawing on the fridge. I would refer him." The second was awareness. "A toddler who falls down knows he has fallen down. He looks around. Sometimes he cries," Mrs. Paulsen said. "He does not get up and explain to you, in complete paragraphs, that he did not fall, that the floor was at fault, and that there are four studies on the subject, three of which do not exist." The third was scale. "A toddler falls down in one living room. Nobody has put a toddler in every living room in the country at once and asked him to answer the email."

Mr. Paulsen, who said he had heard the objections before, "mostly at this table," was not persuaded. If the technology was, in his wife's terms, developmentally delayed, he said, that was not a reason to yell at it either, and it was if anything entitled to accommodations. Asked whether the toddler comparison was his own, he paused for some time before saying he was fairly confident that it was. He had thought of it in the shower, he said, and had afterward asked a chatbot to "tighten it up," and the chatbot had added the sentence about the refrigerator. "The fridge part wasn't mine," Mr. Paulsen said. "But it's the part that landed."

Not every defender of computer-generated content welcomes the defense. [Lowell Pruett](/wiki/people/lowell-pruett/), founder of [Slop Nation](/wiki/organizations/slop-nation/), a Queens organization that seeks to reclaim "A.I. slop" as a term of pride, said in a telephone interview that the toddler comparison was well meant and "deeply infantilizing." "Our members are adults. Most of them are in their forties," Mr. Pruett said. "We are not asking anyone to be patient with us. We are asking them to be proud of us. A toddler grows out of falling down. We have no intention of growing out of anything." He said that Slop Nation had issued a formal response to Mr. Paulsen's post, which runs to ninety-one pages and was released four minutes after the post went up, and that the organization's revised sanitation plan for its proposed march on Skillman Avenue remained before a Queens community board committee that meets again on Nov. 18.

Before the interview ended, Mr. Paulsen fixed one more print to the refrigerator, an image of a toddler in a sunlit living room taking what its caption described as his first steps. The toddler had three legs and was walking well on all of them. Mrs. Paulsen considered it for a while. She said that the child's balance was good, for a child with three legs, but that a single picture was not what she would be looking at. "With a child, you're looking for progress," she said. "You want to see him falling down less in March than he did in November." Mr. Paulsen said that the technology would be walking by spring. He has said so, his wife noted, every spring since 2023.
