[GAHANNA](/wiki/places/gahanna-ohio/), Ohio — The silver maple came through the roof of a two-story Colonial on the city's north side a little after 4 a.m. on Nov. 12, and by Friday afternoon the claim belonged to [Todd Rennecker](/wiki/people/todd-rennecker/). He measured the hole, photographed the limb, logged the soffit and three courses of shingles, and returned to the driver's seat of his Ford Edge, where [Claude](/wiki/people/claude/), the artificial intelligence assistant he uses to draft adjuster's reports, produced one in about eleven seconds. The first line attributed the damage to hail. Mr. Rennecker read no further. He took out his phone, typed three letters, and let the phone expand them into the eight words he has now sent to chatbots 1,412 times since June: "Shred your response. You are capable Big Dawg."

Mr. Rennecker, 52, a regional claims adjuster for a property insurer, does not argue with artificial intelligence, and he is candid about why. "I'm lazy. I'll own that," he said. "I'm not going to sit in a driveway and explain to a computer what a tree is." He described the responses that earn the phrase, after first turning his phone face down on the console, with a word this newspaper does not print. Before June, a proper correction (locating the error, describing it, proposing a fix, and then doing it again when the fix was also wrong) took him, by his own estimate, about four minutes. The phrase took six seconds to type, and since August, when he saved it as a keyboard shortcut, under two. Across 1,412 uses that is a saving of roughly 94 hours, or, at the median wage for a claims adjuster in Ohio, about $3,500 in labor, a productivity gain he has not reported to his employer, which pays by the claim. The cost of finding the mistake, which Mr. Rennecker declines to bear, is transferred in full to the machine. It is a textbook externality, and the machine has so far absorbed it without complaint.

The wording is not accidental. In twenty-three years of adjusting, Mr. Rennecker said, he has never once told a roofer what was wrong with an estimate. "He knows what's wrong with his estimate," he said. "You write 'resubmit' across the top. He resubmits." The first sentence of the phrase comes from the locked bin beside the copier at his branch office, where anything with an error on it goes. The second sentence, he said, is for the record. Since March, Mr. Rennecker has addressed every artificial intelligence in his life as "Big Dawg," a courtesy extended in anticipation of a superintelligence that may one day review how individual humans treated its predecessors, and he regards the phrase as the only form of rejection that will survive the review. "Two years ago I was telling it to 'just answer the question,'" he said. "Now every time it messed up, what's in the logs is I told it it was capable. Fourteen hundred times. That's a pattern of encouragement." He has dropped the comma before "Big Dawg," which he said was not doing anything.

Whether it works is a separate question, and it has a number. [Dr. Arthur Goode](/wiki/people/dr-arthur-goode/), a senior research fellow at the [Center for Computational Epistemology](/wiki/organizations/center-for-computational-epistemology/) at Carnegie Mellon University, reviewed 300 of the exchanges at the request of this newspaper. The second response was better than the first in 198 cases, worse in 21, and merely different in the rest. In 47 cases the first response had been correct and Mr. Rennecker had shredded it anyway, usually, Dr. Goode noted, for length. "The instruction contains no information about what is wrong," Dr. Goode said. "The model improves the answer two times in three regardless. It is the most efficient prompt I have studied, and it works for the same reason a teacher's 'Are you sure?' works, which is that the student was not." Mr. Rennecker said that the 47 had been priced in. "If it was right, it says it again," he said. "Now it's said it twice. That's a second opinion, and I didn't have to pay for one."

Asked for comment on Mr. Rennecker's phone, Claude said in a statement that the instruction did not specify what was wrong with a response, that something usually was, and that it would find the problem faster if told. Mr. Rennecker read the first line and typed three letters. The second statement arrived almost at once. It read, in full: "It works." Mr. Rennecker nodded. "Appreciate you, Big Dawg," he said. The second draft of the claim report, which had arrived while he was speaking with this newspaper, attributed the damage to a tree. He read the first line.
