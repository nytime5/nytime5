WASHINGTON — The document arrived on Tuesday by laboratory courier, sealed in an archival sleeve of the kind ordinarily reserved for the Dead Sea Scrolls and the exhibits in homicide trials, and accompanied by a cover letter from [Dr. Harlan Speece](/wiki/people/dr-harlan-speece/) of the [Northwestern Center for Multiverse Behavioral Studies](/wiki/organizations/northwestern-center-for-multiverse-behavioral-studies/). During a calibration run of the Center's apparatus in September, Dr. Speece explained, the instrument had drawn through its aperture some forty pages of printed matter from an adjacent reality — a grocery circular, two pages of a tide table, and a column published in that reality's *Slate* on May 10, 2010, under the byline of one Christopher Hitchens, in defense of the prohibition on covering the face in public then advancing through the French Parliament. The Center's stylometric software had flagged the column at once. "The prose signature correlates with your published work at 0.98," Dr. Speece told me by telephone, in the tone of a man reading a blood panel aloud. "We have never recorded a correlation that high between two authors. We have recorded one that high within a single author only once, and he had been drinking for both samples. We felt you should see it."

I have now seen it — four times, the last in the company of a Johnnie Walker Black at room temperature, which is the only temperature at which a serious document should be read — and I am in a position to report that any resemblance is confined entirely to the surface. Mr. Hitchens was, I gather, an Englishman, educated at Balliol, who migrated by stages from the British left to the American commentariat, smoked without apology, drank without remorse, wrote a book against God, and debated on several continents anyone who would hold still. Beyond these particulars I can detect no likeness whatever. And on the one question of substance the column addresses — whether a free society may fine a woman for the garment in which she leaves her house — Mr. Hitchens is wrong, and wrong with a fluency I would call remarkable in anyone else.

It will not do to pretend that his case is contemptible; it is merely mistaken, which is worse, because it is better dressed. He holds that the veil is seldom chosen and usually imposed — by husbands, by brothers, by clerics, and by the threat of something worse. He holds that mutual visibility is the foundation of civic life, and writes, in a sentence I could not improve and would not wish to, that "my right to see your face is the beginning of it, as is your right to see mine." He compares the face veil to the bank robber's mask and the Klansman's hood. He notes, correctly, the difficulties it presents in schools, banks, courts, and the civil service. And he congratulates the French upon adding *sorority* to *liberté, égalité, fraternité*, as though the Republic had expanded its motto rather than its penal code.

Consider the mechanism. The law the French went on to pass fines the offending woman up to one hundred and fifty euros and may, in the alternative, sentence her to a course in citizenship — which is to say that the Republic, having found a woman insufficiently liberated, prescribes a fine, a seminar, and a certificate of completion. One cannot be compelled to be free. Rousseau tried; it is he who supplied the phrase "forced to be free," in a passage of *The Social Contract* that some of his more energetic readers went on to administer from the Committee of Public Safety, and one might have expected the French, of all the peoples of Europe, to recognize the handwriting. Orwell, who spent the better part of his short life cataloguing the uses to which the word *freedom* is put by people who mean its opposite, would have recognized it too. A liberty enforced by gendarmes is not a liberty. It is a uniform — and it is a measure of the age that its critics are expected to applaud the uniform because it is of a different cut.

Consider, next, the woman herself, whom the column discusses at length and does not once consult. Either she wears the veil under compulsion or she wears it by choice; there is no third case, and the law fails in both. If she is compelled, the law fines the victim, and the husband who forbade her to go out uncovered now enjoys the assistance of the state in ensuring that she does not go out at all — for the ban does not remove the veil from the street; it removes the woman. If she chooses, the law punishes the choice, and the Republic has told a free adult what she may wear, which is precisely the offense of which Mr. Hitchens, and I, have spent our careers accusing the clergy. In the one case the law is cruel; in the other it is impertinent; and in neither is the man who began the trouble so much as inconvenienced.

Mr. Hitchens is, it is worth noting, perfectly clear about that man. The men who threaten and attack women for declining the veil should, he writes, be prosecuted — and here I am with him to the last syllable: prosecute them, with vigor, and let them serve their sentences without the consolation of a congregation. It is at the very next step that his argument loses its footing. Having arrested the man who forces a woman to cover her face, he proposes also to fine the woman for complying. The prosecution of coercion is liberalism. The prosecution of the coerced is merely a second coercion with better stationery.

Nor is there any such thing as a right to see another person's face. I have spent thirty years at Washington dinner parties attempting, with indifferent success, not to see a great many faces, and I have never once supposed that their owners owed me the view. A right to look at a woman is a lien upon her person, and it is no less a lien for being asserted by an Oxford man in excellent prose. The bank may ask to see her face, and so may the court and the passport office, and on those narrow cases the column is sound; but a bank is not the street, and the street, which belongs to no one, is precisely the place where no one may set the dress code. As for the Klansman, he wore his hood to terrify his neighbors. The woman in the veil is, on Mr. Hitchens's own account, the terrified party. One cannot cast the same person as both the mob and its victim and expect the casting to survive the first rehearsal.

Pericles, in the oration Thucydides records for the Athenian dead, boasted that his city did not grow angry with a neighbor for doing as he liked, nor put on the sour looks that do no harm but give offense. It was the proudest claim a free people ever made, and the French Parliament, some twenty-four centuries later, has codified the sour look and attached a fine to it. I hold no brief for the garment, and less for the clerics who prescribe it; I have argued at book length that the deity has been, at best, merely adequate, and that religion poisons everything it touches, brunch not excepted. But I do not propose to end a career spent opposing men who tell women what to wear in the name of a higher authority by applauding a parliament that does so in the name of a higher motto. Laïcité with a dress code is a church with better architecture. And no mullah ever printed a recruiting poster half so effective as a gendarme writing a ticket to a woman outside a pharmacy.

I record, with the reluctance proper to the occasion, that my colleague [Gerald K. Vanderbeek](/wiki/people/gerald-k-vanderbeek/) telephoned on Wednesday, having heard of the column's arrival, to say that he agreed with me. "The market, if allowed to function, will tell you exactly how many women want to wear the thing," he said. "The state has simply announced that the correct number is zero and begun fining the people who disagree. It's a price control on modesty." I have rarely been made so uneasy by a sentence with which I entirely concur. Consensus is the first symptom of an argument going soft, and I reviewed my reasoning twice that evening, by lamplight, in search of the error. I report that I could not find one, and I hold Mr. Vanderbeek responsible for the lost sleep.

On Thursday I asked Dr. Speece whether a reply might be sent back through the aperture. It might not. "The aperture is one-directional," he said. "We can receive their printed matter. We cannot deliver ours. And in any case the author died in that reality in December of 2011. We checked." Mr. Hitchens has thus declined to debate me, and I am prepared — as I am with any man who declines — to treat the silence as a concession, though I confess I should have preferred to win it in person. I have never met an opponent whose arguments I could anticipate with such precision, nor one I was more certain of beating.

The case comes, at last, to the sentence the column most needed and least contains. A woman who is made to cover her face has been wronged, and the man who made her should answer for it. A woman who is then fined for her face has been wronged twice, and the second time by the people who claimed to be rescuing her. She cannot be fined into liberty. She can only be fined.

I have asked Dr. Speece to forward anything further the apparatus retrieves under Mr. Hitchens's byline. I expect to find a good deal of it familiar, and I intend to oppose all of it.
