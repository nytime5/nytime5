TAKOMA PARK, Md. — A retired [Takoma Park](/wiki/places/takoma-park/) title examiner who has been a member in good standing of the [easily annoyed community](/wiki/phenomena/easily-annoyed-community/) since 1997 has formally objected to the word "community," in a four-page letter that the community's governing body accepted last month as a reservation under its charter and then treated as grounds to renew his membership through 2028, according to correspondence reviewed by The New York Time5.

The municipal building at 7500 Maple Avenue carries two names, joined by a dash: the Takoma Park Community Center, which it became on its expansion in the 2000s, and the Sam Abbott Citizens' Center, the name the city gave it in 1991, a year after [Kenneth Ebersole](/wiki/people/kenneth-ebersole/), now 64, bought a bungalow on Tulip Avenue. Residents tend to use one or the other, much as residents of Brussels use either the French or the Dutch name on its street signs. Mr. Ebersole, who spent thirty-one years searching the Montgomery County land records for settlement companies before retiring in 2023, uses the second. "I spent my whole career telling people where their property ended," he said on Sunday, on his porch. "Every lot in this county has a boundary, and it's written down at the courthouse. This city has had one since 1890. Nobody has ever been able to show me where a community ends. You can't search it, you can't survey it and you can't get out of it. Somebody decides you're in, and they're always very pleased about it."

The easily annoyed community is, by most institutional measures, among the best-organized constituencies in the United States. Its governing body, the [National Council of the Easily Annoyed](/wiki/organizations/national-council-of-the-easily-annoyed/), was founded in Washington in 1971 and has 38,612 members in all fifty states, a charter of twenty-six articles and an annual survey whose response rate last year was 91.7 percent. Eligibility is established by a 140-item questionnaire adapted from ISO/TS 15666, the International Organization for Standardization's technical specification for surveys of noise annoyance. The council's version asks how much, "thinking about the last 12 months or so," each item has bothered, disturbed or annoyed the respondent, and an answer of "very" or "extremely" — the two categories the specification designates "highly annoyed" — on any three is sufficient. Mr. Ebersole's file lists seventeen, among them the end-of-cycle chime on clothes dryers and drivers who wave other drivers through four-way stops. The specification treats annoyance as one person's individual reaction to noise, and defines community noise annoyance as "the prevalence rate of this individual reaction in a community." Shown the passage, Mr. Ebersole read it twice. "Then call it a rate," he said. "I agreed to be counted. I didn't agree to be gathered."

Mr. Ebersole's letter is dated Sept. 14, two days after the city held a community meeting on its review of rent stabilization. The letter objects to the word where it appears in Article 1 of the charter, which describes the membership as "the easily annoyed community," and asks that the word not be applied to him "in any correspondence, roster, newsletter, census or remark," or, in the alternative, that his membership be terminated. His interlocutors at the council treated the letter as what Article 9 calls a reservation: a statement by which a member excepts himself from a provision, permitted so long as it is "not incompatible with the object and purpose of the Council," a test borrowed nearly word for word from the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, which the United States signed in 1970 and has never ratified. By the council's count, members had entered 4,118 reservations between the adoption of Article 9 in 2004 and September, most of them to the dues schedule and the remainder chiefly to the typeface of the newsletter. None had been entered against Article 1.

In an eleven-page memorandum dated Oct. 9, the council's general counsel, [Naomi Kurtzberg](/wiki/people/naomi-kurtzberg/), found the reservation admissible. The council's object and purpose under Article 2 is "to represent persons who are easily annoyed, and to see that their annoyance is recorded," and a member annoyed by the charter itself was, she wrote, not incompatible with that purpose but "squarely within it." The memorandum further noted that the membership office scores all incoming correspondence against the questionnaire's five-point scale, that it had scored each page of Mr. Ebersole's letter "extremely," and that the letter therefore met the standard for membership on its own. On that basis, the council renewed his membership through Dec. 31, 2028. The alternative request was not reached. "Mr. Ebersole wrote that a community has no boundary, and that he therefore could not know whether he was in one," Ms. Kurtzberg said by telephone. "This one has a boundary. It is the top two categories of a five-point scale, and his letter is in the top category on every page. I could not have declined to renew him consistent with the charter. I would add that I did not want to."

His new card arrived on Oct. 20. In accordance with the reservation, it describes him as "Member, Easily Annoyed." "I asked them to take one word off," Mr. Ebersole said. "They took it off, and they gave me two more years." He said that, had he been asked, he would have scored the letter "very." The retirement of the word from an institution's vocabulary has precedent, though seldom at the request of a single party. The European Economic Community, established by the Treaty of Rome in 1957, became the European Community under the Maastricht Treaty in 1993 and was formally replaced by the European Union when the Treaty of Lisbon took effect in December 2009. The word now survives principally in the European Atomic Energy Community, which the treaties left in place.

The [Center for Nonparticipation Studies](/wiki/organizations/center-for-nonparticipation-studies/) in Washington has maintained for most of a decade a standing seminar on whether an explicitly undertaken refusal to participate constitutes a participation. [Dr. Cassandra Murch](/wiki/people/dr-cassandra-murch/), a senior fellow there, said the memorandum was the first answer to that question she had seen in writing from a body with the authority to give one. "Most of the people I interview cannot tell me when their refusal began. The subject can give me the date, the article and the page count," Dr. Murch said. "I do not believe, on the data currently available, that he can leave by any door they would not record as an entrance. I am not certain he would want to, though I would not put that to him."

"People assume a community like ours must be unpleasant to run. It is the easiest thing in the world," said [Evelyn Garvey](/wiki/people/evelyn-garvey/), 71, who has chaired the council's Montgomery County chapter since 2012 and lives four doors from Mr. Ebersole on Tulip Avenue. "Nobody waits to be asked what's bothering them, so there's no small talk, and nobody expects to agree. Ken being annoyed by the word is perfectly normal." The council has reviewed the word itself once, in 2019, when a working group surveyed the membership on fourteen possible replacements and found each of them more annoying than the original; "folks" was rated very or extremely annoying by 93 percent of respondents. "Without the word," Ms. Garvey said, "we're twenty-three people in the Lilac Room who are annoyed separately." The chapter meets in that room, on the second Tuesday of each month, in the building on Maple Avenue, which Ms. Garvey calls the community center and Mr. Ebersole does not. The parties have yet to reach consensus.

Under Article 9, a reservation is circulated to the full membership, any of whom may object within ninety days; objections do not undo a reservation, but they are recorded beside it permanently. Mr. Ebersole's was circulated on Oct. 12. By Friday the council had received 1,114 objections, more than had been filed against all 4,118 previous reservations combined. The period closes on Jan. 10. Mr. Ebersole asked to be sent a copy of each, and the first two hundred arrived on Saturday. On Sunday afternoon, two days before Tuesday's elections, with a yard sign on every lawn on his side of Tulip Avenue but his own, he sat on the porch and read them in the order received. He said they were, on the whole, well argued. Shortly before dark he went inside and came back out with a pad of lined paper.
