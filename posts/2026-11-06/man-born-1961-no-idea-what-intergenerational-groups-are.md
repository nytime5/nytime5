![Duane Ziebell, 65, at his kitchen table in Wausau, Wis., on Thursday, reviewing a list of the American generations that his granddaughter prepared for him in September. Credit: Tom Brueggeman/The New York Time5](man-born-1961-no-idea-what-intergenerational-groups-are.jpg)

WAUSAU, Wis. — The list is, by any measure, a careful document. It occupies one side of a sheet of wide-ruled notebook paper and was drawn up in September, in purple gel ink, by [Duane Ziebell](/wiki/people/duane-ziebell/)'s granddaughter, who is 15 and who had been asked to write down "all of them, in order, with the years." Seven generations are listed, from the Greatest to Alpha, each with its span of birth years and a gloss of two or three words: "Silent — worked hard, didn't complain." In the left margin, in the flat gray of a carpenter's pencil, Mr. Ziebell has added his own notes over the nine weeks since: a question mark beside the Baby Boom, a second question mark beside Generation X, a check mark beside the Greatest Generation, which he has identified as his father's, and, beside the word "Alpha," the words "the little ones?" At the bottom, also in pencil, is an eighth entry the granddaughter did not supply: "JONES (54–65)." The sheet lives on the kitchen table of his split-level on the east side of [Wausau](/wiki/places/wausau/), weighted with a coffee mug, between the morning's *Wausau Daily Herald* and a plastic pill organizer, and he has consulted it, by his own estimate, every morning since it was written. On the refrigerator behind him hangs the city's published snowplow schedule for the 2026–27 season, which he does not need to consult, and does not.

Mr. Ziebell, 65, was born in March 1961 and retired in 2024 after thirty-eight years as an equipment operator for the city's Department of Public Works, the last twenty-nine of them on the same east-side plow route. He knows which streets on that route are cleared before which other streets, and why. What he does not know, of what he calls, consistently and in the face of correction, "the intergenerational groups," is how many there are, what they are for, or which one is his. "I've been told I'm in three of them," he said on Thursday morning, having cleared his own driveway of the season's first snow, an inch and a half that fell overnight, before sunrise. "Every time, the person telling me seems happy about it. Nobody's ever asked me."

The informants, in order, were his grandson, then 12, who said "OK, boomer" to him at Thanksgiving dinner in 2019 after Mr. Ziebell expressed a view on the thermostat, and which Mr. Ziebell took for some years to be a term of affection, "like 'buddy'"; his son-in-law, a middle school guidance counselor in Weston, who informed him in 2023 that anyone born after 1960 was "basically Gen X" and that he should be proud of it; and a retired insurance underwriter at the Rotary pancake breakfast in August, who told him he was neither, but was instead a member of something called Generation Jones, and who then had to leave. "I asked him if the Joneses were in it," Mr. Ziebell said. "There's a Jones family over on Sixth Street. He said it wasn't them." Until this year, he said, he had understood Generation X to be "something on cable," and the millennials to be people born around the year 2000, "from the name." He has since been corrected on both points. He said he accepts the corrections and does not retain them.

It should be said plainly that each of his informants is correct. The Pew Research Center and the U.S. Census Bureau both place the baby boom between 1946 and 1964, which makes Mr. Ziebell a Boomer. William Strauss and Neil Howe, in *Generations*, their 1991 history of American cohorts, began the generation they called the Thirteenth, and that has since been called Generation X, in 1961, which makes him a member of that, in its first year. The cultural commentator Jonathan Pontell coined the term Generation Jones for Americans born between 1954 and 1965, which makes him a member of that as well, more or less in the middle. He is, depending on which authority one consults, a member of three generations at once. None of them has been in touch.

The matter might have stayed at the kitchen table had it not been for a telephone call on Wednesday evening, the day after the midterm elections, from an interviewer conducting a post-election survey of Marathon County voters on behalf of a national polling firm. The county is, as residents here are accustomed to hearing, a swing county in a swing state, and Mr. Ziebell had by his own count been surveyed fourteen times since Labor Day. Question 31 of 40 asked him to identify the generation of which he considered himself a part, from a list of six. He asked the interviewer to read the list a second time, which she did, and then asked her which one she would put him in. She said she was not permitted to suggest an answer. He said, after a pause, that in that case she could put him down for whichever one was left over. The response was entered as "Don't know/Refused." "I didn't refuse anything," Mr. Ziebell said. "I asked her. That's the opposite."

The case is not unusual, according to [Dr. Pauline Esterhaus](/wiki/people/dr-pauline-esterhaus/), who directs the Cusp Studies Program at the [Center for Cohort Boundary Research](/wiki/organizations/center-for-cohort-boundary-research/) in [Madison](/wiki/places/madison-wisconsin/), and who has spent most of two decades on the roughly 28.9 million living Americans born between 1960 and 1966, a population the Center classifies as "cuspal." In a 2025 survey of 3,812 cuspal adults, the Center found that 61 percent could name their own generation under at least one recognized framework, 23 percent could name it under two, 11.4 percent named a generation to which they could not belong under any framework, and 4.7 percent, a group the Center designates "unaffiliated," could not name one at all. Having reviewed Mr. Ziebell's circumstances by telephone at this newspaper's request, Dr. Esterhaus described him as "a textbook unaffiliated cuspal," and 1961 as the most heavily contested birth year in the literature, the only one claimed by all three frameworks at once. "We have been saying for years that these boundaries were drawn after the fact, by people born comfortably inside them," she said. "Nobody born in 1961 was consulted about 1961. He was three years old when the boom is supposed to have ended, and thirty before anyone told him he had been in something else." She said she did not regard his terminology as an error. "'Intergenerational' describes what happens between generations," she said. "As a description of Mr. Ziebell, it is the most accurate term anyone has applied to him."

It would be easy to conclude that Mr. Ziebell has simply not been paying attention, and he would not dispute it. But a man who spent twenty-nine winters clearing the same streets in the same order may be forgiven for having needed, in place of a generation, only a route. His began and ended at the public works garage, and it was cleared by sunrise in every winter of his career, including the ones in which, as the literature now holds, he passed from youth into middle age as a member of one generation, or two, or three. "Nobody on that route ever asked me what year I was born," he said. "They asked me when I'd be by." In September he checked out Douglas Coupland's 1991 novel *Generation X* from the Marathon County Public Library, read forty-six pages and returned it, having found it to be about some people in Palm Springs who had quit their jobs in order to tell each other stories. He said he did not see himself in it. He had kept his job.

Three doors down, [Robert Pavel](/wiki/people/robert-pavel/), 67, a retired letter carrier who [appeared in these pages in May](/2026-05-05/patriotic-derealization-syndrome-america-i-love-does-not-exist/) after a hospital stay in which he was found unable to locate the country he had spent his life in, said he had never had the slightest difficulty on the generational question. "I'm a boomer. Born in '59. I have always known which one I'm in," Mr. Pavel said from his front step. "It's the country I can't find."

Late Thursday afternoon, after this reporter's questions had run out, Mr. Ziebell drew the sheet toward him and, with the carpenter's pencil, bracketed the three spans that include 1961 — Boomer, X and Jones — and wrote his initials in the margin where the brackets overlapped, in the same small block capitals in which, for thirty-eight years, he signed his route sheets. Then he looked at it for some time. "So that's three," he said. "Is there somebody I'm supposed to tell?"
