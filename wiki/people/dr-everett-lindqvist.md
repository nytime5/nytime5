## Public Biography

**Dr. Everett Lindqvist** is a general surgeon at [Codornices Medical Center](/wiki/organizations/codornices-medical-center/) in [Berkeley, California](/wiki/places/berkeley-california/), where he has practiced since 2006, and the most prominent clinical exponent of [artisanal thinking](/wiki/phenomena/artisanal-thinking/), the practice of reasoning through a task without reference to any device, guideline, checklist or colleague.

Dr. Lindqvist received his medical degree from the University of California, San Francisco, in 1998 and completed his residency in general surgery there in 2003, followed by a fellowship in minimally invasive surgery at Oregon Health & Science University. He has performed more than two thousand laparoscopic cholecystectomies and was, between 2015 and 2021, a regional proctor for [Meridian Surgical Technologies](/wiki/organizations/meridian-surgical-technologies/), training surgeons across Northern California on the Halsted robotic system. He is credited by the company with having introduced the Halsted to four East Bay hospitals, including his own.

His public break with assisted practice dates to a 2024 letter in the *Annals of Operative Practice*, "On the Provenance of a Decision," in which he argued that the modern operating room had achieved "a consistency of outcome purchased with the surgeon's own acquaintance with the case," and asked, in a sentence that has since been quoted on a tote bag, "whose thought was that?" On Oct. 13, 2026, forty-seven minutes into a robot-assisted gallbladder removal, he dismissed the Halsted from the procedure, had it parked against the wall, and completed the operation by what he described to the room as "pure artisanal thinking," narrating each decision aloud. The remainder of the procedure took one hour and 52 minutes. The patient recovered without complication and was discharged the following day.

Dr. Lindqvist lives in the Berkeley hills. He maintains a sourdough starter that predates his residency, owns a set of hand-forged scalpel handles made by a smith in West Oakland that the hospital's sterile processing department has declined to accept, and keeps them in a drawer "for the principle." He removed his smartwatch before the Oct. 13 procedure and has not, as of this writing, put it back on.

## Private Profile

**Voice and sensibility:** Dr. Lindqvist speaks in finished sentences and short paragraphs, as though each remark had been drafted and set aside to proof. He is not a crank. He is an excellent surgeon who has decided that the quality of a decision includes where it came from, and he applies the vocabulary of provenance to thought with complete sincerity: single-origin, small-batch, hand-reasoned, encountered. He never says "slow." He regards the word as the finance office's.

**What he notices:** The moment at which a decision is made and by whom. The instruments. The hands. He is more interested in the gallbladder he has in front of him than in gallbladders in general, and considers the general case a form of forgetting.

**Blind spots:** He does not register that the "unassisted" mind he brings to the table is thirty years of training, two thousand prior cases and a residency, all of which were other people's thinking once. He treats "I decided" as a moral category rather than a description. The patient, in his account, is the medium; he is courteous to the medium and would be surprised to be told that it had a schedule.

**Generative tensions:** He trained the region on the machine he now sends from the room. The hospital that employs him is in Berkeley, so its review of him will be thorough and will not conclude. His patients are fine; his cases are long; his colleagues are divided between those who find him insufferable and those who have started narrating. Future stories can follow his extension of the practice into consults, pathology, billing and, eventually, the parking garage.
