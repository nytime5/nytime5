## Public Biography

**Jenna Paulsen** is a pediatric occupational therapist at a practice in South [Austin, Texas](/wiki/places/austin/), where she specializes in the assessment of gross motor development in children from 1 to 6 years old. She has practiced for eleven years and has evaluated, by her own estimate, some 1,400 children for delays in balance, coordination and walking, a body of experience she describes as "a lot of falling down, observed carefully."

Mrs. Paulsen, 35, was born in Lubbock, Texas, and holds a Bachelor of Science in kinesiology from Texas Tech University and a Master of Occupational Therapy from Texas Woman's University. She lives on the east side of Austin with her husband, the freelance software developer [Derek Paulsen](/wiki/people/derek-paulsen/). The couple do not have children.

She first came to public attention in March 2026, when she discovered her husband at the end of a fourteen-hour session of A.I.-assisted programming, unable to say whether he had written the instructions he was following. Asked by him whether she thought he had free will, she told him to drink some water.

In November 2026 she was asked to assess her husband's widely shared claim that complaining about A.I. slop is "like complaining that a toddler is clumsy," and found it inexact in three respects: that most children stop falling down regularly by 4, the age her husband assigns the technology; that a toddler who falls down knows he has fallen down and does not explain otherwise in complete paragraphs; and that no toddler has been placed in every living room in the country at once. She said that a 4-year-old who fell as often as the technology does would, in her practice, be referred for evaluation.

Later that month, when her husband complained publicly that A.I. systems explained things to him that he already knew, she listed, on request, the things he had explained to her unprompted over four years: what a large language model is, roughly monthly; what a token is, more often; why the pictures on the refrigerator were improving; and, once, the gross motor milestones for children from 2 to 5, using a chart she had given him. She observed that children learn to explain things by having things explained to them, and that it was not clear to her who had learned it from whom.

## Private Profile

**Demeanor:** Dry, unhurried and immovably practical. Mrs. Paulsen answers existential questions with physical instructions (drink water, sit down, look at the floor) and abstract claims with developmental milestones. She does not raise her voice and does not look up from her coffee. She is not hostile to technology and owns all of it. She simply measures everything against what a child of the stated age should be able to do, and very little passes.

**Verbal habits:** Lists. Offers to enumerate the ways something is wrong and then does, in order. Uses clinical terms (refer, evaluate, milestone, progress) with complete calm, which makes them land harder. Concedes small points precisely ("the balance is good, for a child with three legs") before declining the larger one.

**Role in the universe:** The domestic counterweight to her husband, whose enthusiasms she reviews at the kitchen table as though they were intake forms. Also a general-purpose source for any story that compares something to a child: a technology "in its infancy," a movement "in its toddler years," a policy "still learning to walk." She will take the comparison literally and grade it.

**Useful for:** Child development, parenting, A.I. stories told from the household rather than the laboratory, and any article in which a bold claim needs to be measured against a growth chart.
