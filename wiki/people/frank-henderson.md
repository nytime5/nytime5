## Public Biography

**Frank Henderson**, 47, is a regional sales manager for a commercial flooring distributor and a resident of [Gahanna, Ohio](/wiki/places/gahanna-ohio/), a suburb that lies beneath the eastern approach to [John Glenn Columbus International Airport](/wiki/places/john-glenn-columbus-international-airport/). He covers a four-state territory and flies, by his own count, between thirty and forty times a year, nearly all of it out of Columbus.

Mr. Henderson came to public attention in October 2026 as the author of a proposal to abolish passenger screening at American airports and replace it with a stainless-steel bowl of knives at the end of every jetway, from which boarding passengers would each take one and to which they would return it on deplaning. The proposal, which he has described as "self-policing," was drawn on the reverse of a boarding pass following a screening at Columbus earlier that month during which he was required to remove his shoes, surrender a bottle of shampoo whose legality he continues to maintain, and observe a gloved hand enter his carry-on bag. He has not submitted the proposal to the Transportation Security Administration, the Federal Aviation Administration or any carrier, preferring, he has said, to "let the idea marinate." The T.S.A. has nonetheless had a copy since the morning he described it to a reporter, the boarding pass having passed through the Concourse B checkpoint in a bin, face up.

Secondary design questions on which he has said he is still working include whether the knives should be serrated and whether passengers in first class should be offered "a smaller, more attractive bowl." Losses would be recovered through a "lost utensil fee" assessed on the passenger's next ticket, a mechanism he has compared to hotel policy on hangers.

## Private Profile

**Voice and sensibility:** Frank speaks in the register of a man who has solved something and is now, patiently, waiting for the rest of the room to catch up. Every objection has already been considered and is, on consideration, fine. He is not angry; he is past anger, in the flat, practical place a frequent flyer arrives at after the four-hundredth removal of his shoes. His sentences are short, declarative, and finished before the listener can object to them. He respects objects that are "real" and distrusts anything "little" or "plastic."

**Obsessions:** The gap between what a system is for and what it does. The dignity of the traveling adult. Mechanisms that run themselves: library returns, hotel hanger policies, honor systems, the quiet discipline of a room in which everyone is equally equipped. He believes most problems are inventory problems.

**Blind spots:** The irrational actor does not exist in his model. Every passenger in a Frank Henderson scenario is a reasonable person who wants to get home, and the knife is simply a tool that person is now holding. He considers a question of mathematics settled when a number has been said aloud. He does not distinguish between a plan and a sketch of one, and would not understand the distinction if it were explained to him.

**Function in stories:** The citizen-engineer — the man whose proposal is wrong in a way that illuminates exactly what is wrong with the system he is replacing. He is at his best quoted at length and taken seriously by a national security correspondent, and he is a natural recurring source for any story touching air travel, federal screening, consumer dignity, or systems that are supposed to regulate themselves. Standing threads: whether the T.S.A.'s "ongoing" assessment of the sketch concludes; whether he ever submits it through the channels that exist; the serration question; the first-class bowl.
