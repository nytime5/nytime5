## Public Biography

**Dr. Rolf Ekelund** is a professor of theoretical physics at the California Institute of Technology in [Pasadena](/wiki/places/pasadena/), and the most austere practitioner of [artisanal thinking](/wiki/phenomena/artisanal-thinking/) yet identified in the physical sciences. He does not use a computer, a collaborator or any result he has not obtained himself, and he will not accept a problem that cannot be solved by one person, at a desk, with a pencil, in what he calls "a human amount of time."

Dr. Ekelund received his Ph.D. from Cornell University in 1991 and joined the Caltech faculty in 1996. For most of his career he was a computational physicist who studied the behavior of electrons in disordered materials and ran simulations on some of the largest machines then available. In 2018 a numerical result that had occupied his group for three years was reproduced in an afternoon by a graduate student in Lausanne using different software, an event he has described as the moment he understood "that neither of us had understood it. The machine had understood it. We had been in the room." He published the result, withdrew from the collaboration, had the computer removed from his office and bought a box of pencils. The Ethernet jack in the office is covered with a square of masking tape. He has published nothing since.

Since 2020 he has maintained a green clothbound ledger listing every result in the history of physics that he judges to have been obtained by a single person working alone with pencil and paper, together with its date and, where he could establish it, the time it took. The entries thicken through the 1920s, a decade he calls "the harvest," and stop in 1930, with the derivation of the maximum mass of a white dwarf star by a nineteen-year-old aboard a steamship between Bombay and Venice. "After that," he has said, "you needed a cyclotron." He has rulings on every proposed later entry: Bell's theorem is mathematics, and Hawking radiation relied on a framework its author did not obtain himself. He is the only member of the appeals panel.

He solves the hydrogen atom most mornings, "to keep the hands in," and had done so 212 times as of October 2026, each solution signed, dated and filed in a drawer. He reads the primary literature in print and receives the newsletter of the artisanal-thinking movement, *The Unassisted*, by mail. He has written to the American Physical Society to ask whether any easy problems remain and received a reply that referred him to a website. He has one graduate student, [Wendy Oyelaran](/wiki/people/wendy-oyelaran/), for whom he has been seeking a thesis problem "her size" since 2022.

The most recent line in the ledger, undated and in pencil, is a problem he has set for himself: to determine, from first principles and by hand, whether any easy problem remains. He expects it to take several years.

## Private Profile

**Voice and sensibility:** Rolf Ekelund is the artisanal thinker as elegist. Where [Dr. Anders Ruhl](/wiki/people/dr-anders-ruhl/) down the corridor is cheerful because every problem looks easy to him, Ekelund is grave because he has concluded, correctly by his own definitions, that every problem that fits through his door has already come through it. He is not a crank and not a fraud: he can solve anything in the ledger, quickly and well, and that is the sorrow. He speaks in short declaratives, as if dictating entries. He is courteous, dry and never raises his voice, because there is no one in his office to raise it to.

**Core position:** A result belongs to whoever obtained it at a desk. Machines, collaborations and apparatus do not obtain results; they produce them, and the distinction is the whole of physics. The 1920s were the last decade in which the universe was within reach of a single pair of hands. Everything since is, to him, not wrong but not his.

**Verbal tics:** "A human amount of time." "A machine, a committee or a hole in the ground." Refers to the 1920s as "the harvest." Describes any assisted result as something its author "did not obtain himself." Says "that is a different thing from hard" about problems that are impossible rather than difficult.

**Obsessions:** The ledger. The exact date on which the easy problems ran out, which he has fixed at 1930 and defends against appeal. Where other people are getting their easy problems: he regards surgeons, who have one every morning, with the particular envy of a man who has none.

**Blind spots:** He cannot see that "easy" is a judgment made after the fact; that the problems in his ledger looked impossible to the people who solved them; or that his own ability to solve the hydrogen atom in fifty minutes is a century of other people's thinking, absorbed. He has not noticed that his graduate student is doing numerics in her car, and would not know how to notice, having no email.

**Generative tensions:** His search for an easy problem is itself a problem, which he has now entered in the ledger, and it has no stopping condition. He can be deployed wherever a story needs someone to grieve for a lost frontier with total sincerity and total accuracy. He pairs naturally with Ruhl (who has infinite easy problems and keeps offering him one), with [Dr. Helene Marquardt](/wiki/people/dr-helene-marquardt/) (who will explain, precisely, why he is right and why it does not matter) and with [Dr. Franklin Krause](/wiki/people/dr-franklin-krause/), who has him counted. Future stories: he finds an easy problem and discovers someone solved it in 1931; the ledger is audited; Ms. Oyelaran's Honda is discovered.
