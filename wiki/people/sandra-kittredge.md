## Public Biography

**Professor Sandra Kittredge** teaches criminal law and health law at the University of Texas School of Law, where she has been on the faculty since 2004. Her scholarship concerns the points at which the two fields meet: the constitutional law of prison and jail medical care, competency to stand trial, involuntary commitment and the financing of care for people in state custody. She is the author of *Deliberate Indifference: Fifty Years of Estelle v. Gamble* (University of Texas Press, 2026), a history of the 1976 Supreme Court decision holding that the Eighth Amendment requires the state to treat the serious medical needs of the people it confines.

Professor Kittredge received her J.D. from Tulane University and clerked for a judge of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit before practicing for six years as a civil rights litigator representing incarcerated plaintiffs in Texas and Louisiana. She has testified before the Texas Legislature on jail health standards on four occasions.

She has been the principal outside legal commentator on [Remand Health](/wiki/organizations/remand-health/), the [Tyler, Texas](/wiki/places/tyler-texas/), company whose uninsured clients steal from its storefront in order to be confined in the county jail for medical treatment. She has reviewed the company's board resolution opposing thefts from its premises and found it sound.

### Notable Statements

- "The Eighth Amendment is the only provision of the Constitution that guarantees an American a doctor, and it has an eligibility requirement. They've read the eligibility requirement."
- On Remand's legal structure: "The entire enterprise depends on its customers committing a real crime against a company that wants them to."

## Private Profile

**Voice and sensibility:** Precise, dry and unshockable. Sandra Kittredge has spent twenty years explaining to students and legislators that the only Americans with a constitutional right to medical care are the ones the state has locked up, and she has long since stopped expecting anyone to find this as strange as she does. She treats Remand as she would a well-drafted brief: she checks the reasoning, finds it correct, and says so. Her assessments are professional, never indignant. The indignation is left in the facts.

**Verbal habits:** Distinguishes carefully between a loophole and a rule ("They haven't found a loophole. They've read the rule."). Uses "eligibility" with audible quotation marks. Grades legal reasoning the way she grades exams, and will say when something would have failed in her class and been right anyway.

**Useful for:** Any story touching constitutional health law, prison and jail medicine, criminal procedure in Texas, or the gap between who is owed care and who receives it. She is the expert who can confirm that an absurd arrangement is legally airtight.

**Avoid:** Making her an advocate for or against Remand. She is a scholar of the obligation. Remand is, in her view, a very good illustration of it.
