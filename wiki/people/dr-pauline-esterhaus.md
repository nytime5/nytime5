## Public Biography

**Dr. Pauline Esterhaus** is a demographer and the director of the Cusp Studies Program at the [Center for Cohort Boundary Research](/wiki/organizations/center-for-cohort-boundary-research/) in [Madison, Wisconsin](/wiki/places/madison-wisconsin/), where she has worked since 2008. Her research concerns the roughly 28.9 million living Americans born between 1960 and 1966, a population the Center classifies as "cuspal," and in particular the minority among them who cannot name a generation of their own under any recognized framework.

Dr. Esterhaus holds a Ph.D. in sociology from the University of Wisconsin–Madison. Her dissertation, *Retrospective Assignment: The Post Hoc Construction of American Birth Cohorts, 1951–1991*, argued that every generational boundary in common use was drawn after the people on either side of it had been born, by analysts born comfortably inside one or the other. She is the author of *Born on the Line: Cuspal Identity in the American Middle Cohorts* (University of Chicago Press, 2017) and the principal investigator on the Center's 2025 survey of 3,812 cuspal adults, which found that 4.7 percent of them — the group the Center designates "unaffiliated" — could not name a generation at all.

She regards 1961 as the most heavily contested birth year in the literature, the only one claimed at once by the Census Bureau's baby boom, by the generation William Strauss and Neil Howe began in 1961 and that is now called Generation X, and by Generation Jones. In November 2026, at the request of *The New York Time5*, she reviewed the circumstances of [Duane Ziebell](/wiki/people/duane-ziebell/) of [Wausau](/wiki/places/wausau/) and described him as "a textbook unaffiliated cuspal." "Nobody born in 1961 was consulted about 1961," she said. Asked about Mr. Ziebell's habit of calling the generations "the intergenerational groups," she said it was "the most accurate term anyone has applied to him."

Dr. Esterhaus was born in Sheboygan, Wisconsin, in 1972, a year assigned to Generation X by every framework the Center tracks, a fact she mentions when asked and regards as a disqualifying privilege.

## Private Profile

**Voice and sensibility:** Weary, exact and on the side of her subjects. Pauline Esterhaus has spent two decades explaining that the boundaries are arbitrary to an audience that wants to know which side of them it is on, and she has stopped expecting either side to listen. She is fond of the unaffiliated in the way a cartographer is fond of an unsurveyed parcel: it is the only part of the map that is honest about itself. She never tells a subject which generation he belongs to. She tells him how many claim him.

**Verbal tics:** "Claimed by," never "belongs to." "Drawn after the fact." Gives the size of a cohort to the tenth of a million. Calls the frameworks by their authors' names rather than their generations' ("under Strauss–Howe," "under Pew"). Treats a lay misnomer as a finding when it happens to be accurate.

**Obsessions:** The year 1961. Prospective versus retrospective classification. The question, open at the Center since 2015, of when Generation Alpha ends. The polling industry's practice of recording a cuspal respondent's uncertainty as a refusal, which she considers a measurement error and has written to three firms about.

**Blind spots:** Her own placement is uncontested, and she is a little too pleased about how much that disqualifies her. She treats being unaffiliated as a kind of integrity and does not always notice that the people she studies would simply like to be told.

**Function in stories:** The standing expert on generations, cohorts, birth-year boundaries, demographic labels and the people who fall between them. Useful in any story where a person, product or policy depends on which generation someone is in, and in any story about surveys that will not accept "it depends" as an answer.
