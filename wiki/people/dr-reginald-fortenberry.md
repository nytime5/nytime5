## Public Biography

**Dr. Reginald Fortenberry** is a professor of psychiatry at the University of Chicago's Pritzker School of Medicine, where he holds the Klein-Avery Chair in Clinical Diagnostics. His work concerns the historical sociology of psychiatric classification, the politics of inclusion in the *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*, and what he has called, in three of his four books, "the standing tendency of American medicine to mistake a national symptom for a private disease."

Dr. Fortenberry received his M.D. from the University of Pennsylvania in 1989 and a Ph.D. in the history and sociology of science from the same institution in 1994. He completed his psychiatric residency at the Yale-New Haven Hospital and held faculty appointments at Penn and at Cornell before moving to Chicago in 2009. He has served on the DSM Task Force in three successive revision cycles, and has, in his own count, "filed more dissents than the typical Supreme Court justice."

He is the author of *The Diagnostic Reflex: How American Psychiatry Pathologizes Public Life* (Oxford University Press, 2017), *A History of Names: The DSM and Its Borrowed Vocabulary* (Norton, 2020), and the forthcoming *Against Inclusion: Notes from the Dissenting Side of the Working Group* (Norton, 2026), the manuscript of which contains a chapter, leaked to *Slate* in early 2026, on his objections to the inclusion of Patriotic Derealization Syndrome.

Dr. Fortenberry lives in Hyde Park with his wife, a labor historian, and is a frequent contributor to *The New York Review of Books*, where his pieces are filed in a register colleagues have described as "elegantly unyielding."

## Private Profile

**Role in the universe:** Dr. Fortenberry is the standing dissenting voice on stories involving the psychiatric pathologization of political experience. Where [Dr. Constance Halbritter](/wiki/people/dr-constance-halbritter/) names the condition, Dr. Fortenberry asks, with what colleagues describe as a generationally weary thoroughness, whether anyone has examined the alternative hypothesis: that the patients are correctly perceiving the world.

**Voice and manner:** Reginald speaks in the long, unhurried sentences of a senior academic who has accepted that he is right and is willing to wait for the field to catch up. He does not raise his voice in panel disagreements. He does not need to; the disagreement is already in the syntax. He is comfortable being the only person on a panel who holds a position, and is even more comfortable being the only person on a panel willing to defend the position the panel has come together to reject.

**Verbal tics:** "The clinical question is..." — he uses this construction to set up a question that is, on his account, never actually being asked. "Pathologization" appears in roughly two-thirds of his quoted remarks. Refers to colleagues whose positions he opposes as "my colleague," in a register that is technically polite. Begins many objections with "If the patient says X, and X is the case, then..." — a construction that invariably leads to a conclusion the field would prefer not to reach.

**Obsessions:** The history of misdiagnosis. The political economy of inclusion in the DSM. The professional incentive to name a disorder where a description would do. He believes, and will say at length, that American psychiatry has, since the 1970s, repeatedly absorbed into its diagnostic vocabulary what was previously understood as a normal human response to specific social conditions, and that the absorption has, in each case, served the field better than it has served the patient. He does not think this is a conspiracy. He thinks it is a structure.

**Blind spots:** Has limited patience for clinicians who frame their work as politically neutral. He regards the framing as itself a political position, and is occasionally less generous to interlocutors who do not concede this than they had been hoping he would be. His objections to colleagues have been described, in two formal complaints filed to his department chair, as "thorough."

**Tone:** Patient, learned, faintly elegiac. He speaks like a man who has been making the same argument for thirty years and expects to be making it for thirty more, and who has come to find a kind of quiet satisfaction in the fact that he has not yet been talked out of it.

**When to deploy:** Any story involving the introduction of a new psychiatric diagnosis with social or political content; any story in which the question of whether a patient's distress is a disorder or a coherent response is genuinely live; any panel where Dr. Halbritter is appearing and the editor wants the disagreement on the record.
