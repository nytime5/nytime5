## Public Biography

**Duane Ziebell**, 65, is a retired equipment operator for the Department of Public Works in [Wausau, Wisconsin](/wiki/places/wausau/), and, in the classification of the [Center for Cohort Boundary Research](/wiki/organizations/center-for-cohort-boundary-research/), "a textbook unaffiliated cuspal": a person born in a contested birth year who cannot name a generation of his own under any recognized framework. He was born in Wausau in March 1961, a year that the Pew Research Center and the U.S. Census Bureau assign to the baby boom, that William Strauss and Neil Howe assigned in 1991 to the first year of what is now called Generation X, and that falls near the middle of the span Jonathan Pontell named Generation Jones. He is, depending on the authority consulted, a member of all three. He refers to them, collectively and in the face of correction, as "the intergenerational groups."

Mr. Ziebell graduated from Wausau East High School in 1979 and joined the city's public works department in 1986. For the last twenty-nine of his thirty-eight years there he drove the same plow route on the east side of the city, which began and ended at the public works garage and which he cleared, by his own account and that of his former supervisors, by sunrise every winter. He retired in 2024. He lives in a split-level on the east side, three doors from [Robert Pavel](/wiki/people/robert-pavel/), and keeps the city's published snowplow schedule on his refrigerator, though he does not consult it.

He was told he was a Boomer by his grandson at Thanksgiving in 2019 ("OK, boomer," which he took for some years to mean "buddy"), that he was "basically Gen X" by his son-in-law, a middle school guidance counselor in Weston, in 2023, and that he was a member of Generation Jones by a retired insurance underwriter at the Rotary pancake breakfast in August 2026. In September 2026 his granddaughter, then 15, wrote out the generations for him in purple ink on a sheet of notebook paper, which he has kept on his kitchen table and annotated in carpenter's pencil. He checked out Douglas Coupland's *Generation X* from the Marathon County Public Library the same month, read forty-six pages and returned it.

On Nov. 4, 2026, the day after the midterm elections, a post-election survey of Marathon County voters asked him to choose his generation from a list of six. He asked the interviewer which one she would put him in, was told she could not suggest an answer, and said she could put him down for whichever one was left over. The response was recorded as "Don't know/Refused." "I didn't refuse anything," he [told *The New York Time5*](/2026-11-06/man-born-1961-no-idea-what-intergenerational-groups-are/). "I asked her. That's the opposite." The next afternoon he bracketed the three spans containing 1961 on his granddaughter's list and initialed the overlap.

## Private Profile

**Voice and sensibility:** Plain, patient, unbothered and sincerely puzzled. Duane Ziebell is not hostile to the generations; he would simply like someone to tell him which one, once, so that it stays told. He accepts every correction offered to him and retains none of them, not out of stubbornness but because none of them has ever been needed for anything. He speaks in short practical sentences about streets, weather and sequence, and he assumes that any system with a list and a set of years has, somewhere, a person in charge of it.

**Verbal habits:** "The intergenerational groups," always. Asks the person informing him which one they would put him in. Measures things by the route: what comes before what, and when he'd be by. Refers to the people who have placed him in a generation as having "told" him, never as having "explained."

**Obsessions:** Order and sequence. The snow, and the hour by which it should be gone. The sheet of paper on the kitchen table, which he consults every morning as he once consulted nothing on the route.

**Blind spots:** It has not occurred to him that the generations are not administered by anyone, and that there is no one to notify. He believes the polling firm made a clerical error. He has not noticed that, of everyone who has raised the subject with him, he is the only one who has asked the question rather than answered it.

**Open threads:** Whether he tells somebody, and whom. Whether the polling firm calls back. His grandson, now 19, who has not been asked to account for "OK, boomer." The Joneses on Sixth Street, who are not in Generation Jones and have not been told about it either.

**Useful for:** Stories about surveys, polls and forms that require a box to be ticked; demographic categories; generational marketing; Marathon County as a place that knows it is being studied; municipal snow removal. He is a natural subject for [Dr. Pauline Esterhaus](/wiki/people/dr-pauline-esterhaus/), and a gentler counterpart to [Kenneth Ebersole](/wiki/people/kenneth-ebersole/) of Takoma Park, also a cuspal, who objects in writing to being placed in a category without a boundary of record. Mr. Ziebell has been placed in three, each with a boundary of record, and objects to nothing.

**Avoid:** Making him confused in general, forgetful or senile. He knows his route, his town and his family exactly. He is unclear on one subject only. Do not let him settle on a generation.
