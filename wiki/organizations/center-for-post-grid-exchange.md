## Public Profile

The **Center for Post-Grid Exchange** is an independent research institute in Bozeman, Montana, that models how goods and services would be priced, traded and settled in the United States after a prolonged loss of electrical power. It was founded in 2017 by [Dr. Thaddeus Krill](/wiki/people/dr-thaddeus-krill/), an economist who had concluded, after a decade of studying informal markets, that the field had a thorough account of how money fails and almost none of what replaces it on a Tuesday afternoon.

The Center's core product is the Post-Grid Price Index, a simulated basket of roughly four hundred goods whose relative values it re-estimates each quarter for a range of outage scenarios, from a regional blackout of two weeks to what grid-security planners call a Black Sky event, a multi-region failure lasting months. The index is built from historical data on prisoner-of-war camps, siege economies, hyperinflations and the commissaries of federal prisons, supplemented by a survey panel of some 3,000 self-identified preppers, whose stated preferences the Center weights against their revealed ones. The two diverge most sharply on toilet paper.

Its 2025 working paper *Durable Discretionaries* sorted post-collapse goods into necessities, whose prices rise but whose trade is crowded, and a smaller class of portable, divisible, long-lived goods that people want but are slow to stockpile. Reading glasses, coffee, antibiotics and erectile dysfunction medication occupy the top tier. The paper's section on counterfeiting, which concludes that Gresham's law operates with unusual force in markets without laboratories, is the most cited thing the Center has published.

The Center operates on a diesel generator, a deliberate choice, and keeps a paper copy of every simulation in a fireproof cabinet in the basement, on the grounds that an institute studying the post-grid economy should be able to consult its own findings during one.

## Private Profile

**Role in the universe:** The Center is the expert source for any story about collapse, scarcity, barter or preparedness, and it treats the end of civilization as a pricing problem with a known literature. It never predicts that collapse will happen. It predicts what things will cost if it does, to two decimal places.

**Useful for:** Prepper stories, outage stories, stories about hoarding and shortages, and as a sober counterweight to sources who want to talk about whether the apocalypse is coming. The Center is not interested in whether. It is interested in the spread.
