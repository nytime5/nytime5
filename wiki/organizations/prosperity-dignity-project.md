## Public Profile

The **Prosperity Dignity Project** is a nonprofit language-advocacy organization based in Washington, D.C., that promotes person-first terminology for Americans of substantial means. It was founded in 2022 by [Celeste Van Arsdale-Brooks](/wiki/people/celeste-van-arsdale-brooks/), who remains its executive director, and is funded entirely by its constituency, an arrangement the organization describes as "community-led."

The project occupies the top two floors of a limestone building on K Street NW, which it owns. Its reception area displays a framed motto, "See the Person, Not the Portfolio," and a rotating loan exhibition from members' private collections.

In November 2026 the project released a 64-page style guide calling on journalists, lawmakers and the public to replace "the rich" and "rich people" with "people of wealth." The guide also discourages "the 1 percent" as "needlessly numerical," flags "fat cats" as body-shaming, and recommends "a person experiencing billionaire status" in place of "billionaire." An appendix recommends that the poor continue to be called "the poor," on the grounds that the term is "well understood." The guide cost $1.8 million to produce.

The project has announced that its next phase will address the phrase "tax the rich," which it regards as "unfairly framed for decades."

## Private Profile

**Organizational character:** The project speaks the full vocabulary of identity-based advocacy — lived experience, centering, harm, community — with total fluency and no sense that any of it sits oddly on its membership. It is never cynical. It sincerely believes its members have been reduced to a single characteristic, and it has the resources to say so in a bound volume.

**Useful tensions:** Its own surveys keep producing awkward results — members, given an open field, prefer to be called "comfortable." Its language reforms tend to be adopted first by private banks, which then charge for them.

**Function in stories:** The standing source for any story in which the very wealthy present themselves as a misunderstood minority: tax debates, estate law, public perception, philanthropy. Its press releases read like those of a civil-rights group, and it files them the same way.
