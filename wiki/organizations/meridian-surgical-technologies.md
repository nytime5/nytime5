## Public Profile

**Meridian Surgical Technologies** is a medical-device manufacturer based in Minnetonka, Minnesota, with product lines in orthopedic implants, surgical staplers and robotic surgery. Founded in 1987 as a maker of hip and knee prostheses, the company entered robotics in 2019 with the **Halsted**, a four-armed robotic surgical system named for William Stewart Halsted, the nineteenth-century surgeon credited with introducing the rubber glove and the surgical residency. The Halsted is leased to hospitals at a typical annual cost of $2.4 million and has, by the company's count, participated in more than four hundred thousand procedures. Meridian describes it in its literature as "a second pair of hands that does not get tired, does not get distracted and does not have an afternoon."

The company's Patella Implant Division held preferred-vendor agreements with several New York orthopedic practices until January 2026, when it discontinued its New York metropolitan sales territory following the collapse in knee-reconstruction volume that accompanied the death of John Reese. The division's then vice president of regional sales, [Marcus Enright](/wiki/people/marcus-enright/), said at the time that the company had "modeled for normal criminal activity" and not for Mr. Reese's retirement. Mr. Enright now oversees the company's robotics accounts in the western United States.

In October 2026 a Halsted system at [Codornices Medical Center](/wiki/organizations/codornices-medical-center/) in Berkeley, Calif., became the first to be dismissed from a procedure in progress, when the operating surgeon, [Dr. Everett Lindqvist](/wiki/people/dr-everett-lindqvist/), a former Meridian regional proctor, had it undocked and parked against the wall. The company said the system had performed as designed and distinguished, for reporting purposes, between a malfunction and a dismissal. It billed the hospital for the full session.

## Private Profile

**Function in stories:** The house surgical-device company, the fictional maker to reach for when a story needs an implant, a stapler, a robot or a vendor agreement. It is competent, unglamorous and Midwestern. It models demand carefully and is periodically undone by a variable it did not think to include.

**Tone and posture:** Meridian speaks in incident codes, modeling language and the vocabulary of availability. It does not defend its products' judgment; it defends their presence. The Halsted did not fail; it waited. The company regards being paid for the waiting as the natural order of things, and is faintly puzzled by anyone who does not.

**Useful tensions:** The Halsted is excellent and its most prominent opponent is the man Meridian paid to teach people to use it. The company's product is availability, which means it profits identically whether the robot operates or stands against the wall, an arrangement that will eventually come to the attention of someone's finance office. Future stories can draw on the Halsted's other dismissals, the Patella Implant Division's recovery, and Mr. Enright's modeling.

**Avoid:** Making the robot sinister or incompetent. The Halsted works. The comedy is institutional, not mechanical.
