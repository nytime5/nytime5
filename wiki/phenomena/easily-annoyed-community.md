## Public Profile

The **easily annoyed community** is the institutionally recognized constituency of Americans who are easily annoyed, and is, by most institutional measures, among the best-organized constituencies in the United States. Its governing body, the [National Council of the Easily Annoyed](/wiki/organizations/national-council-of-the-easily-annoyed/), was founded in Washington in 1971 and has 38,612 members in all fifty states, a charter of twenty-six articles and an annual survey whose response rate in 2025 was 91.7 percent. Article 1 of the charter describes the membership as "the easily annoyed community," and for institutional purposes the two are the same body. Article 2 commits the council "to represent persons who are easily annoyed, and to see that their annoyance is recorded."

Membership is a matter of record rather than of temperament. Eligibility is established by a 140-item questionnaire adapted from ISO/TS 15666, the International Organization for Standardization's technical specification for surveys of noise annoyance. The council's version keeps the specification's five-point scale and asks how much, "thinking about the last 12 months or so," each item has bothered, disturbed or annoyed the respondent; an answer of "very" or "extremely," the two categories the specification designates "highly annoyed," on any three items is sufficient. It omits the specification's qualification "when you are here at home," most of the items being encountered elsewhere. Forty-one of the items are sounds, among them the end-of-cycle chime on clothes dryers and elevators that announce the floor the passenger has just selected; the remainder include drivers who wave other drivers through four-way stops and a turn signal left on for more than a mile. By this standard, a respondent moderately annoyed by all 140 items does not qualify, and one highly annoyed by three and untroubled by the rest does. Researchers distinguish annoyance, a reaction, from noise sensitivity, a trait, and the instrument measures only the first.

The word is older in the research field than in the community. In the acoustical literature, "community annoyance," the proportion of people living near an airport, rail line or highway who report being highly annoyed by its noise, has been a measured quantity since the social surveys of aircraft noise in the 1960s. ISO/TS 15666 treats annoyance as one person's individual reaction to noise and defines community noise annoyance as "the prevalence rate of this individual reaction in a community." In the field, accordingly, a community of the annoyed is a percentage rather than a membership. The field has not kept the word everywhere: the World Health Organization, whose 1999 guidelines on the subject were titled *Guidelines for Community Noise*, titled its 2018 guidelines *Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region*. Survey researchers prize the community's members as respondents, and the field has disputed since at least 1987 whether studies that happen to sample them should report them separately.

The council was founded the year before Congress passed the Noise Control Act of 1972, and the community acquired its present standing among American institutions over the following two decades, principally by converting complaints into reports. A complaint asks for a remedy and is answered, if at all, by the party complained of; a scored report asks only to be recorded, and can be received by anyone. Institutions found the second kind easier to accommodate, and the accommodations the community has won are, with few exceptions, accommodations of record: advance written notice, in 214 counties, of scheduled tests of outdoor warning sirens; a five-point scale on the noise-complaint forms of at least nineteen airports; and the opportunity, now general among American public bodies, to submit comment in writing in lieu of attending a meeting, which members count among the community's earliest accomplishments. Its principal accommodation of the other kind, the setting that silences the end-of-cycle signal on most clothes dryers sold in the United States, has not removed the chime from the questionnaire, because the dryers are delivered with the signal on.

Members are, by the account of the institutions that deal with them, precise rather than loud. A member's annoyance arrives with its object named and, as a rule, asks only to be recorded. "People assume a community like ours must be unpleasant to run. It is the easiest thing in the world," [Evelyn Garvey](/wiki/people/evelyn-garvey/), who has chaired the council's Montgomery County chapter since 2012, has said. "Nobody waits to be asked what's bothering them, so there's no small talk, and nobody expects to agree." Disagreement among members is accordingly frequent, thorough and without consequence.

The council has reviewed the word "community" once, in 2019, when a working group surveyed the membership on fourteen possible replacements and found each of them more annoying than the original; "folks" was rated very or extremely annoying by 93 percent of respondents. The word is defended within the community on two grounds: that every alternative is worse, and that it does what the questionnaire cannot. The questionnaire establishes that each member is annoyed; the word establishes that they are annoyed together. "Without the word," Ms. Garvey has said, "we're twenty-three people in the Lilac Room who are annoyed separately."

In 2026 the name drew [its first reservation](/2026-11-02/member-easily-annoyed-community-annoyed-by-word-community/). In a four-page letter dated Sept. 14, [Kenneth Ebersole](/wiki/people/kenneth-ebersole/), a retired title examiner in [Takoma Park](/wiki/places/takoma-park/), Md., and a member in good standing since 1997, objected to the word where it appears in Article 1 and asked that it not be applied to him "in any correspondence, roster, newsletter, census or remark," or, in the alternative, that his membership be terminated. The letter stated that a community has no boundary, and that he therefore could not know whether he was in one. The council treated it as a reservation under Article 9 of its charter, which permits a member to except himself from a provision so long as the reservation is "not incompatible with the object and purpose of the Council," a test borrowed nearly word for word from the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties. Members had entered 4,118 reservations since Article 9 was adopted in 2004, most of them to the dues schedule and the remainder chiefly to the typeface of the newsletter. None had been entered against Article 1.

In an eleven-page memorandum dated Oct. 9, the council's general counsel, [Naomi Kurtzberg](/wiki/people/naomi-kurtzberg/), found the reservation admissible, a member annoyed by the charter itself being, she wrote, not incompatible with the council's purpose but "squarely within it." The membership office, which scores all incoming correspondence against the questionnaire's five-point scale, had scored each page of the letter "extremely," so that the letter met the standard for membership on its own, and the council renewed Mr. Ebersole's membership through Dec. 31, 2028. The alternative request was not reached. "This one has a boundary," Ms. Kurtzberg has said. "It is the top two categories of a five-point scale, and his letter is in the top category on every page." His new card, which arrived on Oct. 20, describes him as "Member, Easily Annoyed."

The reservation was circulated to the membership on Oct. 12, and any member may object within ninety days. Objections do not undo a reservation but are recorded beside it permanently, and by Oct. 30 the council had received 1,114 from other members, more than had been filed against all 4,118 previous reservations combined. The objection period closes on Jan. 10, 2027. Shown the specification's definition of community noise annoyance, Mr. Ebersole read it twice. "Then call it a rate," he said. "I agreed to be counted. I didn't agree to be gathered."

The case has drawn comment from the [Center for Nonparticipation Studies](/wiki/organizations/center-for-nonparticipation-studies/) in Washington, which has maintained for most of a decade a standing seminar on whether an explicitly undertaken refusal to participate constitutes a participation. [Dr. Cassandra Murch](/wiki/people/dr-cassandra-murch/), a senior fellow there, has said that the council's memorandum was the first answer to that question she had seen in writing from a body with the authority to give one. "I do not believe, on the data currently available, that he can leave by any door they would not record as an entrance," she said.

## Private Profile

**What this is for:** The universe's standing constituency for annoyance: an ordinary human reaction given a governing body, the [National Council of the Easily Annoyed](/wiki/organizations/national-council-of-the-easily-annoyed/), and with it a charter, an eligibility instrument adapted from an international standard and a membership office that scores the mail. Any story about a nuisance (a chime, a siren test, a leaf blower, a four-way stop, a word) has a constituency to consult, and any institution has a body that will answer in writing, on time and on a five-point scale. Among the universe's collective identities it is the opposite of [artisanal thinking](/wiki/phenomena/artisanal-thinking/), which has declined to organize. This one has been organized since 1971 and keeps better records than most of the institutions it deals with.

**The comedy:** Institutional seriousness about annoyance, carried through to closure. Membership is a finding that a person has been highly annoyed, and the membership office scores everything it receives, so no expression of annoyance, including annoyance at the community, its charter or its name, can fail to count as evidence of belonging. [Kenneth Ebersole](/wiki/people/kenneth-ebersole/) asked to be excused from the word or else from the membership, and was renewed for two years. Nobody in the loop is cynical and nobody is setting a trap. The council is being correct, courteously, and is glad to have him.

**The members:** Precise and present, never ranters. Members report what has annoyed them, how much and since when, on the scale provided. They do not raise their voices, exaggerate or ask for a manager, and they may dispute the office's score of their own letters (Mr. Ebersole would have scored his "very"). They are not unhappy. They are usually the most contented people in any story they appear in, because their annoyance has somewhere to go. They read objections in the order received and find them, on the whole, well argued. They disagree completely and without heat. Quoted, a member gives the item and the score and stops.

**Avoid:**
- Mocking any real identity community, or any real group's debate over its own name. The joke is the word as institutional vocabulary, the habit of calling any aggregate a "community." This one is an administrative constituency and must never read as an analogue for a real one.
- Items about who people are. The questionnaire lists devices, procedures, phrases and things people do, such as waving other drivers through a four-way stop. It never lists an identity, a background or a trait, and no member is annoyed by who anyone is.
- Clinical framing. Members do not have a condition. Do not reach for misophonia, hyperacusis or any sensory-processing difference as an analogue, and do not let the questionnaire become a diagnosis. It records reactions over the last twelve months or so, and nothing else.
- Ranting, entitlement, the demand for a manager. Members do not raise their voices. An annoyance is stated and recorded, and the meeting moves on.
- Breaking the loop. Do not let the council expel or refuse a member for being annoyed by it, retire the word, or settle whether a member to whom the word does not apply is in the community. Whether a member can leave at all should stay an open question.
- Remedies. The community's annoyances are recorded, tabulated and accommodated. They are seldom abated, and stories should not abate them.

**Growth paths:**
- The close of the objection period on Jan. 10, 2027: the final count, the objections filed permanently beside the reservation, and whether the membership office, which scores all incoming correspondence, renews the objectors on the strength of their objections.
- Mr. Ebersole's replies to the objections, should he send them, and their scores.
- Further reservations against Article 1, now that one has been admitted. The likeliest is a reservation against "easily," from members who regard their annoyance as earned.
- Other chapters. With members in all fifty states, any local story has a chapter to call. [Evelyn Garvey](/wiki/people/evelyn-garvey/)'s Montgomery County chapter, which meets in the Lilac Room on the second Tuesday of each month, is the only one yet to appear in a story.
- Friction with every other institution that uses the word: the building at 7500 Maple Avenue in [Takoma Park](/wiki/places/takoma-park/), which Ms. Garvey calls the community center and Mr. Ebersole does not; community meetings, among them the city's Sept. 12 meeting on its review of rent stabilization, two days before he wrote; New York's community boards, where [Rosalind Ferrante](/wiki/people/rosalind-ferrante/) has established that her committee does not rule on the meaning of words; and "the international community," a phrase in most of the communiqués [Tariq El-Amin](/wiki/people/tariq-el-amin/) reads, which no one has yet scored.
- The research field, for which a community of the annoyed is a rate and not a membership: a federal survey of noise annoyance that samples members, or declines to.
- The [Center for Nonparticipation Studies](/wiki/organizations/center-for-nonparticipation-studies/): its standing seminar, the paper [Dr. Cassandra Murch](/wiki/people/dr-cassandra-murch/) may write about the memorandum, and the question she would not put to Mr. Ebersole. The natural pairing is [Brendan Vossmeier](/wiki/people/brendan-vossmeier/), also of Takoma Park, whose failure to register to vote is, in his own account, an act of resistance; Mr. Ebersole's letter is, in the council's account, a qualification for membership.
